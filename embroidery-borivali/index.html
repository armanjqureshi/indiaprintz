How embroidery works, step by step

Embroidery builds your logo out of thread instead of ink. A machine stitches the design directly into the fabric, colour by colour, following a pattern prepared from your artwork. Here is the process from start to finish:

  1. Digitising. Your logo is converted into a stitch file that tells the machine where every stitch goes, which direction it runs and which thread colour to use.
  2. Hooping. The garment is held tight in a hoop with a backing layer behind it, so the fabric stays flat and does not stretch while it is stitched.
  3. Stitching. The machine sews the design thread by thread, changing colours as it goes.
  4. Finishing. Loose threads are trimmed, the extra backing is removed and each piece is checked before packing.

Because the design is made of thread, embroidery has a raised, textured feel that no print can copy. It also has limits worth knowing. Very fine text and photos cannot be embroidered, since a thread can only get so thin and a stitch cannot blend colours the way ink can. When to choose embroidery, and when printing is the better pick

Embroidery is premium and long lasting, but it is not right for every design. This quick check helps you decide.

Embroidery is the right pick when:

  • Your logo has clean shapes and a handful of solid colours
  • The garment is a polo, shirt, cap, jacket or hoodie
  • The piece will be worn and washed often, such as a staff uniform
  • You want a formal, premium look for clients or customers
  • The design is chest sized, rather than a large full back artwork

Printing may suit you better in some cases. If your design is a photo, has gradients or relies on tiny text, DTF printing in Borivali reproduces it in full colour and is great for small quantities. For a large back design on event tees, printing is lighter and more comfortable, since a big block of dense stitching can feel heavy and stiff on a t-shirt. For 100 or more festival tees with a simple design, screen printing is usually the most economical.

You can also mix methods, such as an embroidered chest logo with a printed design on the back. Tell us what you have in mind and we confirm the quote on WhatsApp.

Artwork and logo tips for embroidery

Embroidery turns your logo into thread, so a design that looks perfect on screen may need small changes to stitch cleanly. These tips help it come out crisp.

  • Simplify where you can. Fewer colours and bolder shapes stitch more cleanly. A logo with 3 to 4 solid colours usually looks better than one with many shades.
  • Watch the small text. Taglines, phone numbers and fine lettering often become unreadable at chest size. Consider dropping the tagline or printing it elsewhere.
  • Send the cleanest file you have. A vector file such as AI, PDF or SVG is ideal. A sharp, high resolution PNG also works.
  • Expect gradients to become solid shades. Thread cannot fade smoothly from one colour to another, so soft gradients are turned into flat colour areas.
  • Name your thread colours. Tell us your brand colours, or send a reference, and the mockup shows the thread shades on your chosen garment colour.
  • Pick the placement. Left chest is the classic spot for polos and shirts, the front panel suits caps, and a sleeve or the back yoke adds a subtle second mark.

Think about contrast too. A white or gold logo looks lovely on navy, black and maroon, while darker thread stands out on white, grey and light blue garments.

Fabric pairing for embroidery

Embroidery needs fabric with enough body to hold dense stitching. Very thin or very stretchy fabric can pucker around the logo, so firmer materials give the neatest result.

  • Polos in matty, cotton, polycot or bio-wash are the most popular base, firm enough for a clean chest logo.
  • Corporate shirts in cotton or polycot hold embroidery well and keep a crisp, office-ready look.
  • Caps in cotton, polyester or polycot have a stiff front panel that shows a raised logo at its best.
  • Track jackets and varsity jackets give the stitches plenty of support, perfect for crests and initials.
  • Zipper hoodies suit a chest logo, and the thicker fabric keeps the embroidery flat.

For thin t-shirts, a small logo can work, but printing is usually the better choice for anything larger. If you have a fabric weight in mind, tell us the GSM you want and we confirm availability.

Durability and wash care for embroidered garments

Embroidery is one of the longest lasting ways to decorate a garment. There is no print to crack or peel, and good thread holds its colour through countless washes, which is why clinic and office uniforms so often choose it. Many embroidered polos look presentable long after the fabric around the logo has started to show its age.

A little care keeps the stitches looking neat:

  1. Wash the garment inside out to protect the stitches from rubbing.
  2. Use cold or lukewarm water and a mild detergent.
  3. Avoid bleach, which can discolour both thread and fabric.
  4. Iron from the reverse side, ideally over a towel so the stitches keep their raised shape.
  5. If a loose thread ever appears, snip it carefully instead of pulling it.

Staff uniforms get washed far more often than event tees, so a simple care note on the staff noticeboard can add months to their life.

Can you embroider a logo with very small text?

Very fine text cannot be embroidered clearly, because thread can only get so thin. We usually suggest dropping the small text, enlarging it, or printing that part with DTF instead.

Can a photo be embroidered on a shirt?

No, photos cannot be embroidered. Thread cannot blend colours the way ink does, so a photo is best printed with DTF, which handles full colour and fine detail.

Is embroidery or printing better for office uniforms?

For polos and shirts worn every day, embroidery is usually the better choice. It looks more formal and holds up well to frequent washing. Printing suits event tees and large designs.

Does embroidery pucker or shrink after washing?

On firm fabrics like polos, shirts, caps and jackets, embroidery stays flat and neat. Wash the garment inside out in cold water and iron from the reverse side to keep the stitches looking crisp.
