How embroidery works, from your logo to a stitched badge

Embroidery is different from every print method because nothing is printed at all. Your logo is built from thousands of tiny stitches of coloured thread, sewn directly into the fabric. Here is how a logo travels from your phone to a polo.

  1. Digitising. Your logo is converted into a stitch file. This file tells the embroidery machine where every stitch goes, in what direction and in which thread colour.
  2. Hooping. The part of the garment to be embroidered, such as the left chest or the front of a cap, is held firmly in a frame with a backing layer behind it, so the fabric stays flat and does not pucker.
  3. Stitching. The machine sews the design colour by colour, building up fills, outlines and lettering.
  4. Finishing. Loose threads are trimmed, excess backing is removed from the inside and every piece is checked before packing.

The result is raised, textured and rich, with a slight sheen that print cannot copy. Embroidery starts from ₹45 per position. Our main embroidery page explains the method further, and all of it applies to orders from Thane.

Embroidery or printing: how to decide

Embroidery is the right call when the garment will be worn often, seen by customers and expected to look professional for a long time. It suits logos with clear shapes and a limited set of colours, placed at a modest size on the chest, sleeve or cap front.

There are a few cases where printing serves you better:

  • Photos, gradients and very fine text. These cannot be embroidered. Thread works in solid colours, so a photograph or a soft colour fade simply does not translate. DTF printing in Thane handles this kind of artwork in full colour.
  • Large designs across the back. A full back design in embroidery is heavy and stiff on a t-shirt. For big event artwork, printing is lighter and more comfortable.
  • Very large, low budget runs. For 300 event tees with one simple logo, screen printing in Thane brings the cost per piece right down.
  • Light, stretchy round neck tees. Dense stitching can pull at thin fabric, so a printed logo usually looks neater on everyday tees.

A simple way to think about it: embroidery for the garments that represent you every day, printing for the garments made for one big day.

Preparing your artwork for logo embroidery

A logo that looks perfect on a website sometimes needs small adjustments before it can be stitched. These tips help us get it right first time.

  • Send the cleanest version you have. A vector file (AI, EPS, PDF or SVG) is ideal, and a high resolution PNG also works.
  • Simplify where you can. Bold shapes and clear lettering stitch beautifully, while hairline details can disappear.
  • Keep text a readable size. Very fine text cannot be embroidered cleanly, so a tagline under your logo may need to be dropped or enlarged for a chest logo.
  • Tell us your exact colours. We match thread shades as closely as possible and show them on the mockup.
  • Mention the placement. Left chest, right chest, sleeve, back of the neck or cap front all change how large the logo can be.
  • Expect solid colours. Gradients are turned into flat thread tones, and we show you that version on the mockup before stitching.

The mockup step matters even more for embroidery than for print, because it is your chance to see the logo size and thread colours on the actual garment colour. Take your time with it.

Fabric pairing for embroidery

Embroidery is happiest on fabrics with some weight and structure. Matty and cotton polos hold stitches crisply, corporate shirts in cotton or polycot give a smart, flat surface, and caps, hoodies and jackets support even slightly larger logos without sagging.

Polycot is a practical choice for uniforms worn daily, since it keeps its shape and colour through regular washing. Pure cotton feels soft and natural for office polos. Polyester track jackets look sporty with a stitched crest on the chest. If your company has a preferred fabric weight, tell us the GSM you want and we confirm availability.

Durability and wash care for embroidered garments

Stitched thread does not crack, peel or rub off the way some prints can over time, which is why embroidery is so popular for uniforms. With a little care, an embroidered logo can last as long as the garment itself.

  • Wash inside out on a gentle cycle, in cold or lukewarm water.
  • Keep bleach away from the logo, since it can dull the thread colours.
  • Iron from the reverse side, or place a cloth over the embroidery.
  • If a stray thread appears, snip it carefully rather than pulling it.
  • For structured caps, spot clean or hand wash gently instead of machine washing, so the front panel keeps its shape.
