How screen printing works, from artwork to finished tee

Screen printing is one of the oldest ways of printing on fabric, and the idea is lovely in its simplicity. Ink is pushed through a fine mesh stencil onto the garment, one colour at a time. Here is what happens to your design.

  1. Separating the colours. Your artwork is split into its individual colours. A design in saffron, white and maroon becomes three separate layers.
  2. Making the screens. Each colour gets its own screen, a fine mesh stretched on a frame, coated and exposed so ink can pass only through the shape of that colour.
  3. Printing colour by colour. The garment is laid flat, the screen is lined up over the print position, and a squeegee pulls ink across the mesh. The next colour follows, carefully aligned with the first.
  4. Curing. The printed garment passes through heat so the ink sets firmly into the fabric.

The screens are the setup. Making them takes time and care, which is why screen printing is rarely the best value for 10 pieces. Once they exist, printing the 50th or the 150th tee is quick, and that is what brings the cost per piece down so sharply. Screen printing or DTF: choosing by quantity and design

The question committees ask us most often is which method to pick. The answer depends on two things, how many pieces you need and how many colours your design has.

Screen printing is the better pick when:

  • You need about 50 or more pieces of the same design
  • Your design has 1 to 3 flat, solid colours
  • You are printing on cotton or cotton blends
  • You want a bold, opaque print that reads well from a distance

DTF is the better pick when:

  • You need fewer pieces, or each piece carries a different name
  • Your design is a photo or has gradients and many colours
  • You are mixing several different designs in one small order

For small, colourful runs, DTF printing in Vile Parle needs no setup and handles photographs beautifully. If you are still torn, our detailed comparison of DTF vs screen printing puts the two side by side. For hotel polos with a stitched logo, embroidery is the more premium choice, and for all-over designs on polyester jerseys, sublimation is the way to go.

A popular combination is screen printing for the main volunteer run and DTF for a small core team batch with names. Two methods, one order and one delivery.

Artwork tips for crisp screen prints

Since every colour needs its own screen, screen printing rewards designs that are bold and simple. These tips help your artwork print cleanly.

  • Keep to 1 to 3 colours. Each extra colour means another screen and another pass. A strong two colour design often looks better from a distance anyway.
  • Use solid shapes, not gradients. Soft fades and photographic shading do not suit screen printing. If your design depends on them, DTF is the better route.
  • Send vector files. An AI, EPS, PDF or SVG file gives the sharpest edges. A high resolution PNG works if that is all you have.
  • Name your colours. If the shade matters, like the mandal's own saffron, share a Pantone reference or a photo of last year's tee, and we will show the closest match on the mockup.
  • Plan for contrast. White ink on a dark tee and dark ink on a light tee give the strongest result. Pale ink on a pale tee tends to disappear in photos.
  • Keep lettering readable. Very fine lines and tiny text can fill in with ink. Keep slogans bold enough to read from a few feet away, the distance at which most people will see them.

Fabric pairing for screen printing

Screen printing loves cotton. The ink grips the natural fibres well and the print sits firm and opaque. Cotton, polycot, sinker and bio-wash round neck tees are all happy partners, and cotton or polycot polos work well too.

For festival processions in humid weather, a breathable cotton tee keeps volunteers comfortable for hours of dancing and walking. Polycot holds its shape a little better through repeated wear, which suits committees that wear the same tee across several days of celebrations. If you have a GSM in mind, tell us and we confirm availability.

Polyester sportswear is a different story. For polyester jerseys and kits, sublimation is the natural match, so tell us if part of your order is for a sports day and we will suggest the right method for that part.

Durability and wash care for screen printed tees

A properly cured screen print is tough. It sits as a solid layer of ink bonded to the fabric and handles regular washing well, which is why so many families in these old societies still have procession tees from years ago tucked in their cupboards.

To keep yours looking good:

  • Wash inside out in cool water.
  • Avoid bleach on the printed area.
  • Dry in the shade, especially dark tees, so the fabric colour stays rich.
  • Iron on the reverse side, never directly on the print.

After many, many washes, a thick print can begin to soften and show fine lines. That is a natural sign of a well loved tee, and following the steps above keeps that day a long way off.
